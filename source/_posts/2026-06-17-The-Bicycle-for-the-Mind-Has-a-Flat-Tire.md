In the beginning, computers, the web, and apps were built for people to explore,
learn, connect, and make things. They were the "bicycle for the mind," so to
speak.

That is not how a lot of modern computing feels anymore.

The modern equivalents of many of my favorite technologies are now heavy,
user-hostile, restrictive, slow, and addictive. The bicycle for the mind is
still in there somewhere, but it is buried under endless advertisements,
pointless features, restrictive operating systems, long load times, and software
that seems to care more about engagement and control than usefulness.

This is enshittification, and I am tired of it.

## The Breaking Point

For me, the recent breaking point was the iPhone 17 release, combined with
Apple's continued developer- and user-hostile position that the iPhone cannot
run unsigned apps.

Every Mac ships jailbroken; the iPhone should too.

That is the part I cannot get past. Apple already sells a computer that lets me
write a program, compile it, run it, share it, break it, fix it, and learn from
it. That computer is the Mac. The iPhone is also a computer, but it is treated
like it belongs to someone else, even after I buy it.

I understand the security argument. I do not accept that it should be the only
argument. A user-owned computer should have a user-controlled path for running
software outside a company-controlled store.

## My Response

My response is not to wait for the industry to become less annoying. My response
is to build my own versions of the software and services I want to use, with
only the features that actually work for me.

And because I apparently like doing things on hard mode, I am especially
interested in proving that many of these capabilities are possible on hardware
that is more than 10 years old. I want these projects to work on machines like
my iMac G4 and my iPhone 5.

That sounds unreasonable only because we have become used to unreasonable
software. A modern iOS app being hundreds of megabytes is treated as normal now.
So are loading spinners, network dependencies, tracking, account requirements,
subscription prompts, and layers of UI nobody asked for.

I do not think old computers are the problem. I think modern software is often
too bloated to respect them.

## Why AI

AI is not the point of this series. It is the leverage.

These are projects I would not have time to build without AI Coding Assistants.
Or if I did build them, they would be much simpler. AI lets me express myself
more quickly. It helps me get through the tedious parts of programming so I can
spend more time deciding what the software should actually be.

This is especially useful when working in older languages and older development
environments. Mac OS X Tiger does not support Automatic Reference Counting, so
Objective-C means Manual Reference Counting. Doing MRC by hand is a pain. With
AI helping, it becomes much easier to manage.

The same applies to a lot of modern syntax sugar. Objective-C 2.0, Swift, and
SwiftUI are all great when coding by hand. But when an AI assistant is helping
with the mechanical parts, older systems start to feel less limited than they
look. The capabilities are often still there. They are just more annoying to
program for.

That annoyance used to be a hard stop. Now it is more like friction.

## What This Series Is

This series is going to be about using AI Coding Assistants to unenshittify my
own computing life. That means building smaller tools, replacing services I do
not like, reviving older hardware, and making software that reflects what I
actually want instead of what a platform owner or growth team wants.

It is a rant, but hopefully a measured one.

I am not trying to argue that everyone should use an iMac G4 or an iPhone 5.
Most people should not. I am trying to argue that the old idea of personal
computing is still worth defending. Computers should be personal. They should be
inspectable. They should be programmable. They should be fast enough to feel
direct. They should help people make things.

The bicycle for the mind does not need another ad network, another subscription
screen, or another locked door.

It needs a repair stand, a trash can, and someone willing to start taking things
apart.
